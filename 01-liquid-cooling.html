Tutorial 01

Liquid cooling test bench

Liquid cooling is a relay race: heat conducts out of the chip, convects into the moving coolant, then rides away with the flow. This page lets you change the players and watch the finish-line temperature.

The model in three equations

The coolant warms as it absorbs the heat load — sensible heating governed by its mass flow and heat capacity:

ΔTcoolant = Q / (ṁ · cp)   ṁ = ρ · V̇

Heat crossing the wall into the fluid meets a convective resistance set by the heat transfer coefficient and wetted area; the coefficient itself comes from the flow through the Nusselt number:

Rconv = 1 / (h · A)    h = Nu · k / D   turbulent: Nu = 0.023·Re^0.8·Pr^0.4

Stack the resistances in series — package, base conduction, convection — and add the coolant's own rise to get the junction temperature:

Tj = Tinlet + Q·(Rjc + Rcond + Rconv) + ΔTcoolant/2

Simplified educational model: representative coolant properties and a fixed cold-plate geometry. Single-phase uses Fourier conduction plus a Nusselt correlation (laminar Nu = 4.36, turbulent Dittus–Boelter); two-phase uses critical-heat-flux limits. Illustrative, not a design tool.

Three things to try

Cross into turbulence. On water, raise the flow from 1 toward 4 L/min. Around Re ≈ 2300 the regime flips to turbulent, the heat transfer coefficient jumps, and the junction temperature falls — but each extra litre buys less than the last.

Starve the convection. Switch to dielectric single-phase at the same settings. Its low conductivity inflates the convective resistance and the junction temperature climbs hard — which is exactly why dielectrics are rarely used in plain single-phase cold plates.

Find the cliff. Flip that dielectric to two-phase. The flow slider greys out — pool boiling is limited by critical heat flux, not flow. Push the heat load until “CHF used” passes 100% and the surface temperature spikes off the boiling curve into burnout.
