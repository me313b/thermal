Materials & how heat moves

Before a coolant ever touches it, heat has to escape the chip by conduction through solids. Which solids, how thick, and how well they touch decides most of the battle.

The four properties that matter

k — thermal conductivity (W/m·K)
How readily a material passes heat through its own body. Copper ≈ 400, aluminium ≈ 240, silicon ≈ 150, stainless steel ≈ 16, water ≈ 0.6, air ≈ 0.026.
cp — specific heat capacity (J/kg·K)
Energy to raise one kilogram by one kelvin. Governs how much heat a mass can store: Q = m·cp·ΔT.
ρ — density (kg/m³)
Combine with cp to get the volumetric heat capacity ρcp, the honest measure of how much heat a given volume holds.
α — thermal diffusivity (m²/s)
α = k / (ρ·cp). How fast a temperature change spreads, as opposed to how much heat moves. Metals spread fast; water stores instead.

Spread vs store

Metals conduct through a sea of free electrons, so they have both high k and high α — they move heat fast. Liquids and ceramics lack that electron sea: water has decent storage (huge ρcp) but low k, so it is a poor conductor and a fine reservoir. This is exactly why cooling is a relay: a metal carries heat to the coolant, the coolant carries it away.

Conduction as resistance

One-dimensional steady conduction follows Fourier's law, and the grouping behaves like an electrical resistance — which lets you stack layers in series:

Q = k·A·ΔT / L   →   Rcond = L / (k·A)   ΔT = Q·R

Two facts fall straight out of that equation. Thin is good (resistance scales with length L), and area is your friend (resistance falls with A). The trap is that a very thin layer of a bad conductor — a film of thermal grease — can out-resist millimetres of copper, because k differs by a hundred-fold. Things to try

The grease tax. Set a thick copper base, then add a 0.05 mm grease layer. That sliver of grease often shows a bigger temperature drop than several millimetres of copper — interface materials, not the metal, usually limit a stack.

Diamond vs copper. Swap a copper spreader for diamond (k ≈ 2000). The drop across it nearly vanishes — which is why diamond and graphite are used as heat spreaders despite the cost.

Area beats thickness. Halve the footprint and watch every layer's drop double. Spreading heat over more area is often cheaper than chasing exotic materials.
