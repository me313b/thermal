Interfaces & boundary conditions

Heat always has to cross boundaries — metal to metal, metal to liquid. These crossings are where most of a design's temperature is won or lost, and where the maths gets its rules.

The three boundary conditions

Any conduction problem needs to know what happens at its edges. There are three ways to state it, and almost every real surface is one of them:

Dirichlet — prescribed temperature
The surface is held at a fixed T. Example: a wall touching a large boiling pool stays near the saturation temperature.
Neumann — prescribed heat flux
A fixed q″ enters the surface. Example: a chip dumping a known wattage over a known area. A perfectly insulated edge is the special case q″ = 0.
Robin — convective (the one liquid cooling lives in)
The flux leaving the surface is set by convection to the fluid: q″ = h·(Ts − Tf). Both the surface temperature and the flux are unknown until you solve it; h ties them together.
Dirichlet: Ts = T0   |   Neumann: −k·(dT/dx) = q″   |   Robin: −k·(dT/dx) = h·(Ts − Tf)

The metal–liquid boundary up close

Right at a wall the fluid does not move — the no-slip condition. So a thin, nearly stationary film clings to the surface, and heat has to conduct across it before the bulk flow can carry it off. The thickness of that thermal boundary layer is what really sets the heat transfer coefficient h: thin layer, high h. Anything that thins it — faster flow, turbulence, smaller channels — improves cooling.

Metal–metal: thermal contact resistance

Two "flat" surfaces pressed together actually touch only at scattered high points; the rest is air-filled gaps. Heat must funnel through those tiny contact spots, adding a contact resistance Rc that has nothing to do with either metal's bulk conductivity. This is why every joint gets a thermal interface material (TIM) — grease, pad, phase-change, or liquid metal — to flood the gaps with something better than air.

The bondline tradeoff

A TIM layer has resistance R = BLT / (k·A), so you want it thin (small bondline thickness). But squeeze too hard chasing a thin line and you risk pump-out, dry spots, and cracked dies. Real designs settle at the thinnest bondline that stays reliable — then pick the highest-k TIM the application allows. Liquid-metal TIMs reach k ≈ 40–70 but conduct electricity, so they need containment.

One number to keep: the Biot number

The Biot number compares internal conduction resistance to surface convection resistance and tells you whether an object cools uniformly or with a steep internal gradient:

Bi = h·Lc / ksolid   Bi ≪ 1: nearly uniform · Bi ≫ 1: surface much cooler than core

Small Bi (a thin copper fin) means the whole part is essentially one temperature — convenient. Large Bi (a thick low-conductivity block under aggressive cooling) means the surface races ahead of the interior and you must treat the temperature as a field, not a single value.
