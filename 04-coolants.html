Coolant reference

From water through thickening oils to liquid metals — what each fluid offers, what it costs you, and a single number that ranks them for forced-convection cooling.

How to read a coolant

Five numbers decide a single-phase coolant's fate. You want high volumetric heat capacity ρcp to carry heat away, high conductivity k to feed the boundary layer, and low viscosity μ so it moves cheaply and turns turbulent easily — plus a wide liquid range (low freezing, high boiling) and the right electrical behaviour.

The Prandtl number bundles the fluid's transport character, and the Mouromtseff number bundles the whole lot into one figure of merit for turbulent forced convection — higher is better:

Pr = μ·cp / k   |   Mo = ρ0.8·k0.6·cp0.4 / μ0.4   Mo shown relative to water = 100

Why viscosity matters so much

The oils make the point. A 5 cSt silicone oil and a 1000 cSt silicone oil are chemically almost the same fluid, but sort the table by μ and you'll see the heavy grade collapses down the figure of merit. High viscosity thickens the boundary layer, suppresses turbulence (Reynolds number falls), and demands far more pumping power for the same flow. That is the central reason water — thin, conductive, high-capacity — beats almost everything that isn't a metal.

Choosing a coolant

Raw performance

Water, every time, where you can tolerate it. Nothing non-metallic competes on ρcp and k together.

Freeze / boil protection

Water–glycol mixes trade some capacity and a lot of viscosity for a liquid range from roughly −37 °C upward. Propylene glycol where toxicity matters.

Electrical safety / immersion

Dielectric fluids (fluorocarbons, hydrofluoroethers) or oils (mineral, PAO, silicone) can touch live electronics. You pay with much lower thermal performance, so designs lean on huge surface area or two-phase boiling.

Ultra-high heat flux

Liquid metals (galinstan, NaK, sodium) have conductivities ten to a hundred times any other liquid and top the figure of merit — at the price of weight, cost, electrical conductivity, and in the alkali metals, violent reactivity.

Two-phase

A low-boiling-point dielectric (FC-72, HFE-7100) or a refrigerant lets you exploit latent heat near a fixed temperature — bounded by critical heat flux (tutorial 06).
